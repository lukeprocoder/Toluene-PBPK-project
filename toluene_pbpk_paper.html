A Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Model of Inhaled Toluene for Occupational Exposure Assessment

Luke Searcy
Dep. Engineering Technology, University of North Alabama
Contact: csearcy2@una.edu
Independent Analysis | Published October 2026
Abstract

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models translate airborne solvent exposure into an estimate of internal dose, complementing the external air measurements used in occupational hygiene. In this work, a five-compartment PBPK model of inhaled toluene was constructed entirely from published physiological, partition, and metabolic parameters and solved with a hand-implemented fourth-order Runge-Kutta integrator. The model simulated a reference 70-kg worker exposed to toluene vapor over a five-day work week and predicted blood and tissue concentrations during and after each shift. Simulated arterial and venous blood displayed the characteristic biphasic uptake and washout of a volatile solvent, while the adipose compartment accumulated progressively across the week (approximately 2.7-fold higher at end-of-week than end-of-day-one), revealing a body burden not evident from any single measurement. Evaluated independently of either occupational limit, the model predicted that the biological limit for toluene in blood (0.02 mg L−1, pre-shift) corresponds to an 8-h airborne exposure of approximately 28 ppm, within about 40% of the airborne limit of 20 ppm. A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis identified alveolar ventilation, cardiac output, and hepatic metabolic capacity as the dominant determinants of predicted blood concentration. The convergence of two independently derived exposure limits supports the internal consistency of the model and illustrates the value of coupling PBPK modeling with occupational exposure assessment and biological monitoring.

Keywords: physiologically based pharmacokinetics; toluene; toxicokinetics; occupational exposure; biological exposure index; reverse dosimetry; inhalation.

1. Introduction

Toluene (methylbenzene, C7H8; molar mass 92.14 g mol−1) is a volatile aromatic solvent used widely in cleaning and degreasing, adhesives, coatings, paints, and printing. Because it is volatile and lipophilic, workers can absorb substantial quantities by inhalation, and toluene is a well-recognized occupational neurotoxicant. Exposure is controlled by airborne occupational limits, for example, an 8-h time-weighted-average Threshold Limit Value (TLV) of 20 ppm recommended by the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH), a Recommended Exposure Limit of 100 ppm from NIOSH, and a Permissible Exposure Limit of 200 ppm from OSHA.

Air sampling characterizes the concentration outside the worker but not the resulting internal dose. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling closes this gap by representing the body as a set of physiological compartments connected by blood flow, so that an inhaled air concentration can be mapped to concentrations in blood and in specific tissues over time. Such models underpin biological monitoring, for instance, the ACGIH Biological Exposure Index (BEI) for toluene in venous blood (0.02 mg L−1, sampled prior to the last shift of the work week), and enable reverse dosimetry, the inference of external exposure from a measured biomarker.

This study develops a compact, fully reproducible PBPK model of inhaled toluene from first principles, using only published parameters, and applies it to three questions relevant to occupational hygiene: (i) how blood and tissue concentrations evolve during and after a work shift; (ii) whether and how toluene accumulates over a five-day work week; and (iii) whether the airborne limit (TLV) and the independently derived biological limit (BEI) are mutually consistent when linked through the model.

2. Methods

2.1 Model structure

The inhaled-solvent PBPK structure of Ramsey and Andersen (1984) was adopted, comprising five compartments: adipose (fat) tissue; liver, treated as the sole site of metabolism; richly perfused tissues (brain, kidney, and viscera); slowly perfused tissues (muscle and skin); and a steady-state pulmonary gas-exchange region linking inhaled air to arterial blood. Each tissue is modeled as a well-stirred compartment in which the venous blood leaving the tissue is in equilibrium with the tissue, so that the tissue concentration equals the venous concentration multiplied by the tissue:blood partition coefficient.

2.2 Governing equations

For the flow-limited tissues (fat, richly perfused, slowly perfused), the rate of change of the amount A of toluene is

dAt/dt = Qt (Cart − Cv,t) (1)

The liver additionally loses toluene to saturable (Michaelis-Menten) metabolism:

dAliver/dt = Qliver (Cart − Cv,liver) − Vmax Cv,liver / (Km + Cv,liver) (2)

Arterial concentration is obtained from the Ramsey-Andersen steady-state gas-exchange solution, valid because pulmonary blood transit is fast relative to tissue dynamics:

Cart = (Qc Cv,mixed + Qalv Cinh) / (Qc + Qalv / Pb:air) (3)

where Qt is tissue blood flow, Qc cardiac output, Qalv alveolar ventilation, Cinh the inhaled-air concentration, Cv,mixed the flow-weighted mixed venous concentration, and Pb:air the blood:air partition coefficient. Cumulative metabolized mass was tracked as an additional state variable to confirm mass balance.

2.3 Numerical integration

The system was integrated with a fixed-step, fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) scheme implemented directly in NumPy (no external ODE library was used). Step-size convergence was verified: the end-of-shift venous blood concentration was identical to five significant figures at step sizes of 0.010, 0.005, 0.002, and 0.001 h, confirming that the reported results are insensitive to the integration step.

2.4 Parameters

All physiological, partition, and metabolic parameters were taken from the published PBPK literature (Table 1). Airborne concentrations were converted from ppm to mass units using the ideal-gas relation at 25 °C (1 ppm toluene = 3.77 mg m−3). Regulatory and biological limits are summarized in Table 2. Where a source reported a range, a nominal value was used and its influence assessed in the sensitivity analysis (Section 3.4).

2.5 Exposure scenarios

A resting reference worker was exposed to a constant airborne toluene concentration for an 8-h shift followed by 16 h of clean air; this diurnal cycle was repeated for five consecutive workdays and followed by a 48-h exposure-free weekend. Simulations were run at 20 ppm (the current TLV), 50 ppm (a former TLV), 100 ppm (NIOSH REL), and 200 ppm (OSHA PEL). Reverse dosimetry was performed by bisection to identify the airborne concentration whose predicted day-5 pre-shift venous blood equaled the BEI.

3. Results

3.1 Single-shift uptake and washout

During exposure at the TLV (20 ppm), arterial blood rose rapidly and approached a plateau as tissues filled, with mixed venous blood remaining below arterial because tissues continued to extract toluene (Figure 1). Arterial and venous concentrations peaked near 0.26 and 0.21 mg L−1, respectively. On cessation of exposure, blood concentrations fell steeply during a fast blood-and-tissue phase and then declined slowly as the adipose reservoir released stored toluene, the characteristic biphasic washout of a lipophilic volatile solvent.

3.2 Accumulation over the work week

Because toluene is highly lipophilic (fat:blood ≈ 65), the adipose compartment loaded progressively across the work week while blood reset each night (Figure 2). Adipose toluene at the end of the Friday shift was approximately 2.7 times its end-of-Monday value (6.6 versus 2.4 mg L−1) and cleared only slowly over the weekend, whereas liver and the perfused tissues returned near baseline each night. This accumulation constitutes a body burden that a single spot air measurement cannot reveal.

3.3 Comparison with the biological limit

The BEI is defined for a pre-shift blood sample near the overnight trough taken before the last shift of the work week; the appropriate model comparison is therefore the day-5 pre-shift concentration rather than a peak. The model predicted a day-5 pre-shift venous blood concentration of 0.014 mg L−1 at 20 ppm (0.68 of the BEI) and 0.036 mg L−1 at 50 ppm (1.8 times the BEI) (Figure 3).

3.4 Sensitivity analysis

A one-at-a-time ±30% perturbation of each parameter showed that predicted blood concentration is governed chiefly by alveolar ventilation, cardiac output, and hepatic metabolic capacity (Vmax), with a ±30% change in ventilation producing the largest response (about ±23%) (Figure 4). The blood endpoint was nearly insensitive to adipose volume and the fat:blood partition coefficient, even though those parameters govern the fat burden in Figure 2, a reminder that parameter importance depends on the output of interest.

3.5 Dose-response and reverse dosimetry

Sweeping the airborne concentration produced a near-linear relationship between exposure and predicted pre-shift blood over the occupational range (Figure 5). Inverting this relationship by bisection gave the reverse-dosimetry result: a pre-shift blood concentration equal to the BEI corresponds to an airborne exposure of approximately 28 ppm, close to, and within about 40% of, the 20 ppm TLV.

4. Discussion

The principal finding is an internal-consistency check between two independently established exposure limits. The airborne TLV (20 ppm) and the blood BEI (0.02 mg L−1) were derived separately and from different evidence. The PBPK model, which received neither as an input, predicts that the BEI corresponds to an airborne exposure of about 28 ppm, the same order of magnitude as the TLV and within roughly 40%. Agreement of this closeness between quantities the model was not fitted to supports the physiological and physicochemical assumptions embedded in it.

The residual gap is itself informative and has plausible physical origins. First, the simulations assume a worker at rest; physical work elevates alveolar ventilation and cardiac output, which the sensitivity analysis (Figure 4) shows would increase uptake and lower the airborne concentration equivalent to the BEI toward the TLV. Second, biological limits are typically set with a deliberate margin of safety. Third, the metabolic parameters (Vmax, Km) and partition coefficients carry real uncertainty across published sources. None of these require the model to be incorrect; rather, they define the most informative refinements to pursue.

From an occupational-hygiene standpoint, the model makes explicit why airborne monitoring and biological monitoring are complementary. The progressive adipose accumulation in Figure 2 shows that, for a lipophilic solvent, weekly body burden is systematically understated by any single air measurement; the quantitative rationale for pairing air sampling with biological monitoring when assessing solvent exposure.

5. Limitations and future work

  • Resting physiology. Incorporating work-rate-dependent ventilation and cardiac output is the single most impactful refinement indicated by the sensitivity analysis.
  • Inhalation route only. Toluene carries a skin notation; adding a dermal-uptake compartment would complete the total-dose picture.
  • Parameter uncertainty. Metabolic constants and partition coefficients should be re-verified against primary sources and, ideally, the model calibrated against a published human chamber-exposure dataset.
  • Metabolite biomarkers. Extending the model to urinary o-cresol and hippuric acid would allow evaluation of the corresponding urinary BEIs.
  • Population variability. Monte-Carlo sampling over parameter distributions would yield predictive ranges rather than a single deterministic trajectory.

6. Conclusion

A compact, fully reproducible five-compartment PBPK model of inhaled toluene, built solely from published parameters and solved with a hand-coded RK4 integrator, reproduced the expected uptake, tissue distribution, adipose accumulation, and clearance of a volatile solvent across a work week. Used for reverse dosimetry, the model linked the airborne TLV and the blood BEI to within about 40%, an independent consistency check that also highlights the complementary roles of air and biological monitoring. The model is a transparent starting point for occupational toxicokinetic analysis and for the refinements outlined above.

Data and code availability

The model and analysis are implemented in two self-contained Python scripts (toluene_pbpk.py, pbpk_analysis.py) that reproduce every figure from the parameters in Tables 1–2 using only NumPy and Matplotlib. No experimental data were generated or used; all inputs are the published constants cited herein.
